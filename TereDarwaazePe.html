Tere Darwaaze Pe Chilman Nahin Dekhi Jaati

Lyrics by Fana Bulandshehri and,
Performed by Ustad Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan

"Tere Darwaaze Pe Chilman Nahin Dekhi Jaati" by Fana Bulandshahri presents love as something that demands openness, intensity, and truth rather than concealment or societal restriction. Through rich imagery—like chilman (veil), zulf (hair), jaam (wine glass), and khaak-e-nasheman (ruined home) — the poet moves from longing and admiration of beauty to a deeper rebellion against barriers, rituals, and judgment. He rejects anything that hides or limits real experience—whether it’s veil in love, societal norms of the shehar, or even symbolic worship over direct perception of jamaal (true beauty). By the end, the tone turns melancholic, revealing the pain of lost love and shattered emotional refuge, suggesting that when love is suppressed or lost, it leaves behind only ruins. Overall, the qawwali reflects a Sufi-like philosophy - true beauty and love must be experienced directly, fearlessly, and fully—otherwise they either suffocate or turn into heartbreak.

Lyrics and Translations

Tere Darwaaze Pe Chilman Nahin Dekhi Jaati,
Jaan-E-Jaan Humse Yeh Uljhan Nahin Dekhi Jaati

"तेरे दरवाज़े पे चिलमन नहीं देखी जाती,
जान-ए-जाँ हमसे ये उलझन नहीं देखी जाती"

"تیرے دروازے پہ چلمن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

"جانِ جان ہم سے یہ الجھن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

( I cannot bear to see the curtain hanging at your door,
O the love of my life, I cannot endure this state of confusion / restlessness )

Word Breakdown & Meanings:
• Chilman ( चिलमन | چلمن ): A thin curtain, veil, or screen—usually hung at doors or windows. In poetry, it symbolizes separation between lovers, barrier or distance (emotional or physical), or something that hides beauty or truth.
• Uljhan ( उलझन | الجھن ): Confusion, complication, or something tangled. In poetry, Uljhan refers to inner restlessness, emotional conflict, or feeling mentally or emotionally stuck or troubled.
Poetic Interpretation:

The opening line Tere Darwaaze Pe Chilman Nahin Dekhi Jaati sets a scene of intimacy and vulnerability. Here, the poet establishes a conflict between his intense desire for a direct connection and the physical or social barriers that prevent it. The Chilman symbolizes more than just a piece of fabric; it represents the frustrating veil of separation, whether it be societal modesty, the beloved’s hesitation, or the metaphorical distance between the seeker and the divine. By calling this sight an Uljhan, the poet suggests that his heart is no longer satisfied with mere proximity or hints of presence; the uncertainty of what is hidden behind the screen creates a restless psychological knot that he can no longer endure. He addresses the beloved as Jaan-E-Jaan (Life of my soul), implying that because she is the very essence of his existence, any barrier between them feels like an unnatural and suffocating obstruction to his own spirit.

Rukh Pe Yeh Zulf Ki Uljhan Nahin Dekhi Jaati,
Phool Ki Godd Mein Naagin Nahin Dekhi Jaati

"रुख पे ये जुल्फ की उलझन नहीं देखी जाती,
फूल की गोद में नागिन नहीं देखी जाती"

"رخ پہ یہ زلف کی الجھن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

"پھول کی گود میں ناگن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

( I cannot bear to see the tangled tresses scattered across your face;
I cannot bear to see a female cobra resting in the lap of a flower )

Word Breakdown & Meanings:
• Rukh ( रुख | رخ ): Face or countenance. In poetry, Rukh often symbolizes the beloved’s face, which is the primary focus of admiration and longing. It can also represent the outward expression of inner beauty and emotion.
• Zulf ( ज़ुल्फ़ | زلف ): Tresses or locks of hair. In poetry, Zulf is a common symbol of beauty, mystery, and allure. It often represents the emotional turmoil caused by longing.
• Naagin ( नागिन | ناگن ): A female snake (serpent). In poetry, it is used as a metaphor for dangerous beauty, hidden threats, or intense passion. The image of a Naagin resting in a flower’s lap evokes a sense of forbidden allure and the tension between attraction and danger.
Poetic Interpretation:

In this couplet, the poet employs a striking and classic Urdu poetic metaphor Tashbeeh to describe the beloved's appearance. He compares the fair, delicate face of the beloved to a Flower and the dark, Zulf to a Naagin. While long, dark hair is usually celebrated as a mark of beauty, the poet views it here as an intruder or Uljhan (tangle) because it dares to obscure the radiance of the face. There is a sense of aesthetic perfectionism here; he feels that the cobra of the hair is too dangerous or dark an element to be allowed to coil around something as pure and soft as a flower. By calling it an Uljhan , he mirrors the restlessness from the first verse, suggesting that even the smallest natural veil—like a stray strand of hair—is an unbearable distraction from the total vision of beauty he seeks.

Be-Hijaabana Milo, Humse Yeh Parda Kaisa,
Band Doli Mein Suhaagan Nahin Dekhi Jaati

"बेहिजाबाना मिलो, हमसे ये पर्दा कैसा,
बंद डोली में सुहागन नहीं देखी जाती"

"بے حجابانہ ملو ہم سے یہ پردہ کیسا"

"بند ڈولی میں سہاگن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

( meet me without any veil, for what is the need for this curtain,
I cannot bear to see a bride trapped inside a closed palanquin )

Word Breakdown & Meanings:
• Be-Hijaabana ( बे-हिजाबाना | بے حجابانہ ): Without a veil or covering. In poetry, it can symbolize emotional openness, honesty in love, or a desire for direct connection without barriers.
• Suhaagan ( सुहागन | سہاغن ): A married women whose husband is alive. In poetry, it often symbolizes the idealized beloved, beauty and grace of a bride, love, union and completeness. Here, it emphasizes the tragic image of a bride trapped in a closed palanquin, unable to be seen or to meet her beloved freely.
Poetic Interpretation:

In this couplet, the poet makes a bold plea for Be-Hijaabana intimacy, questioning the very necessity of the Parda (Curtain) that stands between lovers. By using the metaphor of a Suhaagan inside a Band Doli (a closed palanquin), Fana Bulandshehri highlights a tragic irony: a bride represents the pinnacle of beauty, celebration, and new life, yet tradition dictates she be locked away in a wooden box where no one can see her radiance. He argues that true beauty loses its purpose if it is strictly confined by social decorum or artificial barriers. To the poet, keeping the beloved hidden is not an act of protection, but a stifling imprisonment that denies the seeker the Darshan (Vision) they crave and denies the beauty itself its right to be admired.

Jaam Mein Ho Toh Nazar Aaye Gulaabi Joda,
Humse Botal Mein Yeh Dulhan Nahin Dekhi Jaati

"जाम में हो तो नज़र आए गुलाबी जोड़ा,
हमसे बोतल में ये दुल्हन नहीं देखी जाती"

"جام میں ہو تو نظر آئے گلابی جوڑا"

"ہم سے بولٹل میں یہ دُلھن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

( if it were in a glass, that pink dress would finally be visible,
I cannot bear to see this bride trapped inside a bottle )

Word Breakdown & Meanings:
• Jaam ( जाम | جام ): A glass or goblet, often used in poetry to symbolize a vessel for wine, which itself is a metaphor for divine love, intoxication, or spiritual ecstasy. Here, it represents a transparent container that allows the beauty of the pink dress to be seen clearly.
• Dulhan ( दुल्हन | دلہن ): A bride. In poetry, a Dulhan often symbolizes beauty, love, and the idealized beloved. Here, it represents the wine personified as a bride, whose beauty is wasted when trapped inside a bottle.
• Gulaabi Joda ( गुलाबी जोड़ा | گلابی جوڑا ): A pink outfit or attire. In poetry, the color pink can symbolize love, tenderness, and romantic beauty. Here, it emphasizes the delicate and alluring nature of the wine, which is being compared to a bride dressed in pink.
Poetic Interpretation:

The line here personifies the wine as a Dulhan dressed in a pink outfit, representing its rosy, intoxicating glow. He argues that as long as the wine remains inside the opaque or dark bottle, its beauty is imprisoned and its essence is wasted—much like a bride hidden from her groom. The bottle is a metaphor for any container or social construct that keeps a beautiful thing suppressed. By insisting that it be poured into the Jaam, he is calling for the manifestation of beauty. He wants the hidden to become apparent, the potential to become actual, and the secret to be shared. It reflects a philosophical stance that the divine or the beautiful only finds its true meaning when it is revealed to the eye of the beholder, rather than being kept in cold, dark isolation.

Ghar Banayein Kisi Sehra Mein Mohabbat Ke Liye,
Shehar Waalon Se Yeh Jogan Nahin Dekhi Jaati

"घर बनायें किसी सहरा में मोहब्बत के लिए,
शहर वालों से ये जोगन नहीं देखी जाती"

"گھر بنائیں کسی صحرا میں محبت کے لیے"

"شہر والوں سے یہ جوگن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

( let us build a home in some desert for the sake of our love,
for the people of the city cannot bear to look upon this ascetic )

Word Breakdown & Meanings:
• Sehra ( सहरा | صحرا ): A desert or wilderness, often used in poetry to symbolize a place of isolation, freedom, or spiritual retreat. Here, it represents a space where true love can flourish without the constraints of society.
• Shehar ( शहर | شہر ): A city or urban area. In this context, it represents the conventional world with its societal norms and limitations.
• Jogan ( जोगन | جوگن ): A female ascetic or mendicant who has renounced the world for the sake of her beloved. In poetry, a Jogan symbolizes selfless devotion, spiritual love, and the idea of surrendering worldly attachments for the sake of love. Here, it represents the poet's own soul or the beloved, who is seen as an ascetic figure that the city cannot understand or accept.
Poetic Interpretation:

The poet suggests that the Shehar (the city/civilization) is too narrow-minded and judgmental to understand the depth of true devotion. He refers to the beloved (or perhaps his own soul in love) as a Jogan — a female ascetic or mendicant who has renounced the world for the sake of her beloved. In a city governed by superficial rules, such raw and selfless devotion is often mocked or looked down upon as madness. Therefore, the poet proposes moving to the Sehra (the desert/wilderness). The desert represents a space of absolute freedom and vastness, where there are no walls or prying eyes. By choosing the desert, he is choosing a sanctuary where love can exist in its purest, most un-veiled form, away from the civilized world that is too small-hearted to witness the majesty of a soul surrendered to love.

Hum Nazar-Baaz Hain Dikhla Humein Devi Ka Jamaal,
Moorti Humse Brahman Nahin Dekhi Jaati

"हम नज़र-बाज़ हैं दिखला हमें देवी का जमाल,
मूर्ति हमसे ब्रह्मन नहीं देखी जाती"

"ہم نظر باز ہیں دکھلا ہمیں دیوی کا جمال"

"مورتی ہم سے برہمن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

( we are connoisseurs of beauty show us the radiance of the goddess,
o brahmin, we cannot bear to see merely a silent, cold idol )

Word Breakdown & Meanings:
• Nazar-Baaz ( नज़र-बाज़ | نظر باز ): A connoisseur of beauty, someone with a refined eye for appreciating the true essence of beauty and divinity. In poetry, a Nazar-Baaz is often portrayed as a lover or seeker who can see beyond superficial appearances to the deeper spiritual or emotional truths.
• Jamaal ( जमाल | جمال ): Glory, radiance, or beauty. In poetry, it often refers to the divine beauty of a beloved or a deity. Here, it represents the true, vibrant essence of the goddess that the poet longs to see.
• Moorti ( मूर्ति | مورتی ): An idol or statue, often used in religious contexts to represent a deity. In poetry, a Moorti can symbolize the limitations of physical representations of the divine, which may be revered but are ultimately lifeless and static compared to the living reality of the divine presence.
Poetic Interpretation:

The poet identifies himself as a Nazar-Baaz — one who has the refined eye to appreciate the true essence of beauty and divinity. He addresses the Brahman (the priest or the gatekeeper of tradition) and demands to see the actual Jamaal (glory/radiance) of the Goddess herself. His grievance is with the Moorti (the idol), which he sees as a static, lifeless representation that stands in the way of the living truth. He is arguing that for a true seeker, a symbol is not enough; he craves the vibrant, breathing reality of the divine presence. It is a bold statement of mystical rebellion, suggesting that religious rituals or symbolic representations are veils in themselves, and a true lover of beauty cannot be satisfied until they come face-to-face with the actual light of their beloved.

Ae 'Fana' Keh De Hawa Se Ke Udaa Le Jaaye,
Humse Yeh Khaak-E-Nasheman Nahin Dekhi Jaati

"ऐ ‘फ़ना’ कह दे हवा से के उड़ा ले जाए,
हम से ये ख़ाक-ए-नशेमन नहीं देखी जाती"

"اے ‘فنا’ کہہ دے ہوا سے کہ اڑا لے جائے"

"ہم سے یہ خاکِ ناشمن نہیں دیکھی جاتی"

( o 'fana', tell the wind to come and blow it all away,
I cannot bear to see these ashes of my own nest )

Word Breakdown & Meanings:
• Khaak-E-Nasheman ( ख़ाक-ए-नशेमन | خاکِ نشیمن ): The dust or ashes of the nest. In poetry, this phrase symbolizes the remnants of a home, a life, or a relationship that has been destroyed or fallen apart. It can represent the emotional wreckage left behind after a profound loss or heartbreak.
Poetic Interpretation:

In the concluding verse, the poet Fana reaches a pinnacle of emotional exhaustion and spiritual surrender, using the metaphor of a destroyed nest to signify the end of his earthly attachments. By addressing the wind and pleading for it to blow everything away, he expresses a profound desire for total annihilation of the self (the literal meaning of his pen name, Fana). The Khak-E-Nasheman (the dust or ashes of the nest) represents the remnants of his life, his efforts, and his broken heart; to him, these are no longer symbols of a home, but painful reminders of a struggle that has reached its limit. He finds the sight of these ruins unbearable because they represent a middle state — neither a whole life nor complete nothingness. By calling for the wind to scatter these ashes, he is not just expressing despair, but a mystical longing to be erased entirely, moving beyond the entanglements and veils mentioned in the earlier verses to a state where there is no seeker, no barrier, and no suffering left to witness.
